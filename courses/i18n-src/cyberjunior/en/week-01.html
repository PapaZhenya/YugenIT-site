Summary

Information security is a set of practices and technologies that protect data and systems from unauthorized access, modification, and destruction. The entire subject rests on three properties — the CIA triad.

The CIA triad

  • Confidentiality — data is accessible only to those who are supposed to see it. Violated by leaks, traffic interception, credential theft.
  • Integrity — data is not altered without detection. Violated by file tampering, injections, man-in-the-middle attacks.
  • Availability — the system is available when needed. Violated by DDoS, ransomware, outages.

Any attack can be described by which of these properties it violates — this is the first thinking tool of a security specialist.

Who attackers are. Motive and skill determine the vector: script kiddies (ready-made tools, low skill), hacktivists (ideology), cybercriminals (money, medium-to-high skill), insiders (legitimate access, hard to detect), APT groups (state-sponsored, highest skill).

Threat modeling and STRIDE. A threat model answers four questions: what are we protecting, from whom, what are the consequences, and with what measures. STRIDE is a convenient methodology for systematizing this: Spoofing, Tampering, Repudiation, Information disclosure, Denial of service, Elevation of privilege. The result is a risk matrix, where each threat is scored by likelihood and impact.

Going deeper

Authentication, authorization, accounting (AAA)

Three related but distinct concepts. Authentication — who you are (identity verification: password, key, fingerprint). Authorization — what you're allowed to do (which actions and resources are permitted). Accounting — what you did (logs, audit). They're often confused, but protection is built on all three: first identify, then restrict rights, then record actions.

Vulnerability, threat, risk, exploit

Let's break it down. Vulnerability — a weak point (an unlocked window). Threat — a potential event that exploits it (a thief nearby). Exploit — a specific way to take advantage of a vulnerability. Risk — likelihood of occurrence multiplied by impact. A defender reduces risk by closing vulnerabilities, lowering likelihood, and limiting impact.

Least privilege and defense in depth

Two fundamental principles that will recur throughout the course. Least privilege — everyone gets exactly as much access as they need. Defense in depth — multiple layers, so that one failure doesn't expose everything. Good security isn't a single "silver bullet" — it's layers.

Another example: a threat model for an online store

The asset is the customer payments database. Confidentiality is critical (a leak means a hit to customers and reputation). Threats by STRIDE: disclosure (database leak), tampering (price manipulation), denial (DDoS during a sale). Risk of a database leak is high (likely + major impact) → priority #1: encryption, access control, monitoring. DDoS is medium → overload protection. This is how an abstract "fear" turns into a concrete plan.

Step-by-step walkthrough: build a threat model in 5 steps

  1. Describe the system — what are we protecting (assets: data, services, devices).
  2. Determine value — which part of the CIA triad is critical for each asset.
  3. Find threats — using STRIDE (spoofing, tampering, repudiation, disclosure, DoS, elevation of privilege).
  4. Assess risk — likelihood × impact for each threat.
  5. Plan measures — what to do about priority risks (mitigate, accept, transfer, avoid).

Mini-project: a threat map of your own system

Take a system you know well (your website, app, or home network) and build a mini threat model:

  • a table of assets and their CIA value;
  • 5–6 threats mapped with STRIDE;
  • a 3×3 risk matrix (likelihood × impact);
  • 3 priority protective measures.

This makes a great first item in a security portfolio.

Key terms

  • CIA — confidentiality, integrity, availability.
  • STRIDE — a threat-finding methodology (6 categories).
  • Vulnerability / threat / risk / exploit — see "Going deeper".
  • AAA — authentication, authorization, accounting.
  • APT — a sophisticated, targeted, long-term attack (usually state-sponsored).
  • Least privilege — the minimum access necessary.
  • Defense in depth — multiple layers of defense.

Common mistakes and myths

  • Myth: security is about "hackers in hoodies." In reality, it's about processes, risk, and hygiene.
  • Mistake: confusing threat and vulnerability. A vulnerability is a weak point; a threat is the event that exploits it.
  • Myth: "no one is attacking us." Most attacks are automated and don't pick a specific target.
  • Mistake: chasing a "silver bullet." Defense in depth works — not a single measure.
  • Myth: security gets in the way of work. Good security is proportional to risk and nearly invisible.

Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

  • Where do I start in infosec? With the fundamentals: networks, OS, threat modeling — which is exactly what this course provides.
  • Do I need to know programming? Basic scripting is useful, but early on, understanding systems matters more.
  • What does a SOC analyst do? Monitors events and investigates incidents — the goal of this course.
  • What matters more — attack or defense? For a junior, understanding both sides matters; most people enter through defense (Blue Team).

Lab work

Part A. Asset inventory

  1. Invent a fictional company and build an asset table: website, customer database, payment data, email, accounting.
  2. For each asset, determine which part of the CIA triad is most critical, and justify it.

Part B. Finding threats with STRIDE

  1. For 3–4 key assets, come up with 1–2 threats per STRIDE category.
  2. Formulate each threat concretely: who gains access, how, and to what.

Part C. Risk matrix

  1. Rate the threats by "likelihood" and "impact" (low/medium/high).
  2. Build a 3×3 matrix and highlight the priority threats.
  3. For two priority threats, propose a risk-reduction measure.

Additional lab work

Part D. Analyzing real-world breaches

  1. Find 2 public incidents (data breaches) in the news.
  2. For each, determine which CIA property was violated.
  3. Hypothesize which vulnerability and threat were involved.

Part E. STRIDE analysis of an app

  1. Pick any app you're familiar with (a messenger, a banking app).
  2. Go through the 6 STRIDE categories and come up with one threat for each.
  3. Note which threat is most dangerous for this app and why.
