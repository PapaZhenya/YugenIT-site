Summary

Credentials are an attacker's most sought-after commodity. Most breaches start with a weak password or a single successful phishing email.

Hashes and brute-forcing. Passwords are stored as hashes (a one-way function). An attacker doesn't "decrypt" a hash — they try candidates and compare. John the Ripper and Hashcat work offline against stolen hashes; Hydra brute-forces passwords against live services online.

Reuse is deadly. A breach at one service opens every account that shares the same password (credential stuffing). The fix is a password manager and unique passwords.

Social engineering and phishing. Bypasses technology through the human. Signs of phishing: a mismatched sender address, time pressure, suspicious links, unexpected attachments. MFA neutralizes a stolen password.

Deeper dive

Hashes, salt, and why you can't "decrypt" them

Passwords are stored as hashes — the result of a one-way function. A hash can't be reversed; an attacker can only try candidates and compare. Salt is a random value added to the password before hashing — it makes identical passwords produce different hashes, so precomputed tables become useless. Defense: strong hashing algorithms + salt.

Why password reuse is fatal

One breach, and the attacker tries the same password everywhere (credential stuffing). That's why passwords must be unique. A password manager solves this: it generates and stores unique, complex passwords so you don't have to remember them.

Multi-factor authentication (MFA)

MFA adds a second factor (a code, an app, a key). Even after stealing a password, an attacker can't log in without the second factor. It's one of the most effective and cheapest defenses — it neutralizes most password attacks.

One more example: an SSH brute-force attack (in the logs)

In auth.log, dozens of Failed password lines from a single IP within a minute, followed by Accepted password — a classic brute-force that succeeded because of a weak password. Defense: long passwords, attempt limits (fail2ban), key-based login, MFA. You'll be hunting for this exact trace during the investigation in week 9.

Step-by-step walkthrough: how to recognize phishing

  1. Sender — does the address actually match the real one? (watch for subtle letter substitutions).
  2. Tone — pressure through urgency and threats? A red flag.
  3. Link — where does it actually lead (hover, don't click)?
  4. Attachment — unexpected? Don't open it.
  5. Request — asking for a password/code/payment? Legitimate services don't do that.
  6. In doubt — go to the site directly, not through the link in the email.

Key terms

  • Hash — a one-way function used to store passwords.
  • Salt — a random addition that defeats precomputed tables.
  • Credential stuffing — trying leaked passwords elsewhere.
  • MFA — multi-factor authentication.
  • Phishing — deception used to steal data.
  • Password manager — a vault for unique passwords.

Common mistakes and myths

  • Myth: a hash can be "decrypted". No — it can only be brute-forced and compared.
  • Mistake: one password for everything. One breach = every account compromised.
  • Myth: MFA is unnecessary hassle. It's one of the most effective defenses there is.
  • Mistake: trusting an email based on the sender's display name. The address is easy to spoof.

Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

  • Why can't a hash be decrypted? The function is one-way; only brute-forcing works.
  • Are password managers safe? Yes — far more reliable than reusing passwords or storing them in notes.
  • Does MFA protect against phishing? It sharply lowers the risk: a stolen password alone isn't enough.
  • Where can I practice legally? On your own training VMs; brute-forcing someone else's service is illegal.

Lab work

Part A. Working with hashes

  1. Generate several password hashes of varying complexity.
  2. Crack the weak hashes with John the Ripper using a dictionary (rockyou).
  3. Compare the time it takes to crack a simple versus a complex password.

Part B. Online brute-forcing (your own VM only)

  1. Spin up an SSH service on a training machine.
  2. Brute-force a weak password with Hydra.
  3. Discuss how brute-force protection (fail2ban) would have stopped the attack.

Part C. Analyzing phishing

  3. Put together an 8-point "How to recognize phishing" cheat sheet.
