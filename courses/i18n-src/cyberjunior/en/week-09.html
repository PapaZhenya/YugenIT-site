Summary

Sooner or later, defenses fail — and then everything comes down to how well you respond.

The IR lifecycle. Six stages: preparation → detection → containment → eradication → recovery → lessons learned. The goal is to minimize damage and avoid repeating the same mistakes.

Forensics and evidence preservation. The main rule: capture the state first, then act. Disk images and logs are hashed, and a chain of custody is maintained.

Logs as evidence. The primary material is logs (auth.log, syslog, web logs), command history, and memory dumps. The key skill is not to believe the apparent "end of the story" after history -c, but to reconstruct the picture from multiple sources.

Material: the "Incident #1" dataset (web01) — auth.log, access.log, syslog, command history with traces of the full attack chain. See datasets/scenario1_web01_breach/.

Deeper Dive

The Six IR Stages in Detail

Preparation — plans, access, and tools ready in advance. Detection — noticing the incident. Containment — preventing it from spreading (isolating the host). Eradication — removing the cause (closing the hole, deleting the malware). Recovery — restoring operations. Lessons learned — what to improve. Skipping any stage is costly.

Evidence Preservation (chain of custody)

The main rule: capture first, act second. Disk images and logs are hashed to prove they haven't been altered, and a chain of custody is maintained — who handled the evidence, when, and how. Otherwise the evidence loses its validity. Don't "clean up" an infected machine right away — collect data first.

Indicators of Compromise (IoC)

IoC — traces that reveal an attack: suspicious IPs, hidden files (/tmp/.x), new accounts, cron jobs, unusual processes. Collecting IoCs helps you both investigate the current incident and learn to spot similar ones in the future.

One More Example: Why history -c Doesn't Save the Attacker

The attacker cleared their command history, thinking they had covered their tracks. But their actions are reflected in syslog (user creation, cron), in the web logs (scanning), and in auth.log (login). By cross-referencing sources, the analyst reconstructed the entire chain. Lesson: don't trust a single source — the truth is assembled from several. This is exactly what distinguishes a competent investigation.

Step-by-Step Walkthrough: Log Investigation (web01 Scenario)

  1. Read the incident brief (dataset scenario1_web01_breach).
  2. Find the attacker's IP by cross-referencing auth.log and access.log.
  3. Find the first stage (web scanning) by User-Agent in the web log.
  4. Count the failed logins before the successful one; find the moment of compromise.
  5. Reconstruct the actions from the command history: reconnaissance → theft → persistence.
  6. Build a timeline (time → event) citing the relevant log lines.

Key Terms

  • IR — incident response (6 stages).
  • Forensics — collecting and analyzing digital evidence.
  • Chain of custody — the chain of accountability for evidence.
  • IoC — indicator of compromise.
  • Timeline — the reconstructed sequence of events.
  • Persistence — a mechanism an attacker uses to maintain access.

Common Mistakes and Myths

  • Myth: history -c destroys all traces. Traces remain in other logs.
  • Mistake: cleaning up the machine immediately. Collect evidence first.
  • Myth: one log is enough. The truth is assembled from multiple sources.
  • Mistake: not hashing evidence. Without this, you can't prove it wasn't altered.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

  • Where should the investigation start? With the brief, then cross-referencing sources by time and IP.
  • What matters more — speed or preserving evidence? Contain the spread, but don't destroy the evidence.
  • Where can I get data for practice? The course's ready-made log datasets (weeks 9–10).
  • Is this what SOC work actually is? Largely yes — detecting and analyzing incidents.

Module Cheat Sheet

  • IR: Preparation → Detection → Containment → Eradication → Recovery → Lessons Learned.
  • First capture the evidence (hash, chain of custody), then act.
  • Don't trust a single log — cross-reference sources.
  • Look for IoCs: suspicious IPs, hidden files, new accounts, cron.
  • The end result is a timeline and a report.
