This module walks through the entire journey of a single message, from pressing Enter to the page appearing on your screen — introducing hosts, transmission media, protocols, and packets.

Module goals

  • See the whole data transmission chain — from sender to receiver
  • Get acquainted with the key players in a network: hosts, media, devices
  • Understand why protocols are needed and what a packet is
  • Grasp the difference between "data" and "service wrapper"
  • Learn to spot network exchange in real traffic

Introduction

Let's not start with dry definitions, but with something you do every day: open a website. You press Enter — and a fraction of a second later, the page appears. It seems to happen by itself, in one smooth motion. In reality, during that second your request passes through the hands of a dozen devices, gets split into pieces, travels across half the planet, and gets reassembled. If you understand this whole path right now, half the course will already sit in your head in advance — because from here on we'll simply be slowly unpacking each of its steps.

Notes

Who takes part in data transmission

Any network exchange has at least two participants: the one who sends, and the one who receives. Both are called hosts — this could be your phone, a laptop, a smart kettle, or a huge server in a data center. From the network's point of view they're all equal participants, and each has its own address.

Between hosts there is always a transmission medium — the physical thing the data actually runs through. It's either a copper cable (twisted pair), fiber optics (data travels as light), or radio waves (Wi-Fi, mobile connections). Each medium has its strengths: cable is reliable, fiber is fast and works over long distances, radio is convenient but temperamental.

And finally, the flow is directed by someone. Between your device and the server sit special devices — switches and routers — that decide where to forward the data next. We'll cover them separately; for now just remember that they exist, and that without them a packet would get lost.

Why nothing works without rules

Imagine two people trying to negotiate, but one speaks Japanese, the other Spanish, and both keep interrupting each other. Zero use. It's the same with devices: for them to understand each other, they need a shared set of rules.

These rules are called protocols. A protocol defines everything: what format to pack the data in, how to mark the start and end of a message, how to confirm that everything arrived, what to do in case of an error or loss. There are many protocols, and each is responsible for its own piece of the job — one for delivery between networks, another for reliability, a third for making sure you see a web page.

An important idea: a network is not so much "wires" as it is agreements. Wires merely carry the signal — it's the protocols that give it meaning.

What happens when you press Enter

Let's break down, in broad strokes, what happens during that fraction of a second:

First, your request ("show me the page example.com") turns into a form the network understands. Then it's split into small chunks — called packets. Each chunk is tagged with a destination address — where it should be delivered. The packets fly through a chain of devices toward the server, possibly even by different paths. The server receives them, reassembles them into a whole request, prepares a response (the page itself) — and that response comes back to you the same way, in packets. Your browser assembles everything together and renders the page.

This whole dance takes milliseconds and repeats dozens of times while a single page loads (text, images, styles — these are often separate requests).

What a packet is, and why data gets split

A packet is a small block of data with service information attached (first and foremost, the sender's and recipient's addresses). You can picture it as a postcard: there's text (data) and there's an address on the back (the service part).

Why can't the whole message be sent in one piece? Three reasons. First, reliability: if one huge chunk is lost along the way, everything is gone; but a small lost packet is easy to resend. Second, flexibility: different packets can be routed different ways and dodge congestion. Third, fairness: when everyone splits their data into chunks, the channel is shared among many, and one heavy file doesn't block the network for everyone else.

That's exactly why splitting data into packets is a foundational idea of all modern networks.

Data and wrapper: a first look at headers

Every packet consists of two parts: the data itself (what you're actually transmitting) and a header — a service label with addresses and markings. On the way to the recipient, several such labels get attached to the data — different ones at different stages. On the receiving end, the labels are peeled off in reverse order, and the recipient gets down to the plain data.

For now, just remember this image — "data inside, labels outside" — in module 3 we'll see that each label corresponds to its own network layer, and we'll lay everything out in detail.

Going deeper

Bandwidth, latency, and loss

Network quality is described by three values. Bandwidth — how much data passes per second (Mbps): this is the "width of the pipe." Latency — how long it takes for a signal to travel there and back (ms): this is the "length of the pipe." Loss — what share of packets didn't arrive. Fast internet isn't only about a "wide pipe": for calls and games, low latency and no loss matter more than a huge download speed.

Client and server

Almost everything on a network follows a "client-server" pattern. The client requests (your browser), the server responds (the website's computer). The same computer can be both a client and a server in different situations. Understanding this role will help later: when we say "the server listens on port 443," we mean exactly the side that waits for requests.

Worked example: how a letter reaches its recipient

Let's draw an analogy with regular mail — it turns out to be remarkably accurate.

You write a letter (data). You put it in an envelope and write down the recipient's address and your return address (the header with addresses). You drop it in the mailbox, and the mail carrier picks it up (your device sends the packet). The letter travels through sorting centers, each one checks the address and passes it on in the right direction (routers steer the packet through the network). If there are many letters, they're carried in batches and by different vehicles (splitting and different routes). At the destination, the envelope is opened and the letter is taken out (the recipient strips the headers and reads the data).

Keep this picture in mind for the whole course — almost anything in networking can be explained through it.

One more example: a phone call as a network analogy

Calling a friend is also a "network." A phone number is like an address (where to call). Dialing and "hello, hello, can you hear me?" is like establishing a connection and checking the link. The conversation itself is data. Rules of politeness (don't interrupt, say goodbye) are the protocol. If the connection drops and you call back — that's like resending a lost packet. Keep such analogies handy: almost any networking idea can be explained through a call or a letter.

Step-by-step breakdown: what actually happens in one second

  1. You type in an address and press Enter.
  2. Your device figures out the site's IP (via DNS — we'll cover this in module 2).
  3. The request data is split into packets, each tagged with a destination address.
  4. The packets go to the router, which sends them onward to the provider.
  5. Through a chain of devices, the packets reach the server.
  6. The server assembles the request, prepares the page, and splits the response into packets.
  7. The response packets return to you following the same principle.
  8. The browser assembles everything together and renders the page.

Usually this cycle repeats dozens of times for a single page (text, images, styles — often separate requests).

Mini-project: a map of your home network

Put together a one-page "map" of your home network:

  • draw all the devices and how they're connected (cable/Wi-Fi);
  • mark the router as the central hub and the gateway to the internet;
  • label which devices are "clients," and which might be "servers" (for example, a network printer or a NAS);
  • add a short description: what happens when one of the devices opens a website.

This is your first network document — you'll keep adding to it later.

Key terms

  • Host — any device participating in a network (phone, PC, server) that has a network address
  • Transmission medium — the physical carrier of the signal: cable, fiber optics, or radio waves
  • Protocol — a set of rules by which devices exchange data
  • Packet — a small block of data with a service header, the unit of transmission on a network
  • Header — the packet's service information (primarily addresses) attached to the data
  • Sender / recipient — the hosts between which the exchange takes place

Common mistakes and myths

  • Myth: data travels as one chunk. No — it's almost always split into many packets.
  • Mistake: confusing wires with the network. A network is, first and foremost, protocols (rules), not cables.
  • Mistake: thinking there's always a single path. Different packets of the same message can take different routes.
  • Myth: the internet is one big machine. It's millions of devices, linked by shared rules.

Frequently asked questions (FAQ)

  • Are "packet" and "frame" the same thing? Almost: they're data with different headers at different layers. We'll sort out the exact difference in module 3.
  • Does data really travel different paths? Yes, packets of the same message can travel by different routes and come together at the end.
  • Why does it sometimes happen that "the internet is fast, but the game lags"? Because for games, low latency and no loss matter more than just download speed.
  • Who came up with these rules? International organizations and the engineering community; protocols are the result of shared agreements, not one company.

Module cheat sheet

  • Host — a device on the network. Medium — cable/fiber/radio. Devices — switches and routers.
  • Packet = header (addresses) + data.
  • Data is split into packets for reliability, flexibility, and fair access.
  • Protocol = rules of exchange. Without them, communication doesn't work.
  • Network quality: bandwidth, latency, loss.

Lab work

Part A. Describe the path yourself

  1. Open any website and write out, in your own words, a chain of 5-6 steps: from pressing Enter to the page appearing.
  2. Mark in your chain where your device is, where the home router is, where the site's server is.
  3. Underline the moment where the data gets split into packets.

Part B. Find the participants in your network

  1. Determine which devices at home are connected to the network (phones, TVs, laptops, smart devices).
  2. Find your main network device (the router) and write down its model.
  3. Note for each device how it's connected: by cable or over Wi-Fi.

Part C. Peek at a real exchange

  1. Install Wireshark and start a traffic capture on your active interface.
  2. Open a simple website, wait a couple of seconds, and stop the capture.
  3. Find the packets that went FROM you, and the packets that came back IN RESPONSE (pay attention to the Source and Destination columns).
  4. Roughly count how many packets it took to load one page — make sure it's a lot.

Part D. Build your own "mail analogy"

  1. Draw a diagram: you → router → internet → website server, and label what happens at each stage.
  2. Label on the diagram where the "letter" (data) is, and where the "envelope with the address" (header) is.

Additional labs

Part E. Measure your network

  1. Find an online speed test and measure the bandwidth (Mbps).
  2. Run ping to a popular website and check the latency (ms) and loss.
  3. Compare: is your speed high, but what's the latency like?

Part F. Client and server around you

  1. Make a list of services you use as a client (websites, messengers).
  2. Find devices of your own that act as servers themselves (printer, TV, smart speakers).
  3. Explain why one device can be both a client and a server.
